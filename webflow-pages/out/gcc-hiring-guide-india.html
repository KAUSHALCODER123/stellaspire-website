GCC Hiring in India: The Complete 2026 Guide

Setting up or scaling a Global Capability Centre in India is, above all, a hiring project. This guide walks through the hiring phases, the roles that matter most, how the main hub cities differ, and the mistakes that slow new centres down.

The short answer

GCC hiring in India usually happens in three phases: founding leadership, core functional teams, then scale. Hire the site leader and function heads first, because they shape culture and attract the next hires. Benchmark pay against the local GCC market, plan diversity from the first hire, and choose a hub city based on the talent you need as well as cost.

Key takeaways

  • Hire the site leader and first function heads before the wider team. They shape culture and attract the next hires.
  • Plan hiring in three phases: founding leadership, core functional teams, then scale and new capabilities.
  • Benchmark pay against the local GCC market for the role and city, not against the parent country.
  • Build gender balance from the first hire. It is much harder to correct later.
  • Choose a hub city for the talent you need most, and agree early with the parent who owns each hiring decision.

What is a GCC?

A Global Capability Centre (GCC), sometimes called a global in-house centre or captive centre, is an offshore unit owned by a global company. Unlike an outsourcing vendor, a GCC is part of the parent organisation. It delivers work such as finance and accounting, analytics, technology, research and operations, and increasingly owns end-to-end processes and products rather than simply executing tasks.

That shift from “support centre” to “capability centre” changes who you need to hire. Today’s GCCs look for leaders who can own outcomes, influence global stakeholders and build teams as well as manage delivery.

Why companies build GCCs in India

  • Depth of specialist talent. India offers large pools of finance, accounting, analytics, data and engineering professionals, many with experience in global organisations.
  • An established GCC ecosystem. Many global companies already run centres in India, so experienced GCC leaders and managers exist in the market.
  • Capability as well as cost. Companies increasingly move complex work such as FP&A, controllership, data science and AI to India for expertise as well as efficiency.
  • Time-zone coverage. India sits between the major markets, which helps with follow-the-sun operations.

The three hiring phases of a GCC

  1. Founding leadership

    The first hires set the tone for everything that follows. Typically this means a site head or centre director, plus the leaders of the first functions to move, for example a head of finance operations, a financial controller or an analytics lead.

    These searches are usually confidential and best run as retained searches. Look for people who have built or scaled a centre before, who are credible with the global leadership team, and who can attract strong first-line managers through their own network.

  2. Core functional teams

    Once leaders are in place, hiring shifts to managers and senior individual contributors who will run processes and transitions: FP&A managers, record-to-report and accounts payable leads, data engineers, BI leads and analysts. This is the phase where process knowledge transfer happens, so experience of transitions and documentation matters as much as technical skill.

  3. Scale and new capabilities

    As the centre stabilises, hiring becomes more repeatable and volumes rise. At the same time, mature centres add higher-value capabilities such as decision science, AI and machine learning, finance transformation and centres of excellence. Senior specialist searches continue alongside volume hiring, and many companies use a mix of in-house recruiters and specialist partners.

Tip

Involve your founding leaders in hiring their own direct reports. Candidates often join for the person they will work for, and leaders own the team better when they chose it.

The first ten hires in a new GCC: a sensible order

Every centre is different, but the early sequence tends to follow the same logic. Each hire should make the next one easier. A reasonable order looks like this:

  1. Site head or centre director. The person who owns the centre, represents it to the parent and becomes the face of the employer brand in the local market.
  2. HR lead or talent acquisition lead. Someone who can run hiring, onboarding and local people policies while the team grows.
  3. Head of the first function to move. For example a financial controller, head of finance operations or analytics lead.
  4. Finance and admin support for the entity. Someone to handle local payroll, compliance and vendor set-up, often shared with the parent at first.
  5. First-line managers in the lead function. The people who will receive the transition and run the work day to day.
  6. Senior individual contributors. Subject experts who can document processes and train later hires.
  7. Head of the second function. Brought in once the first function is stable.
  8. Technology or systems lead. Someone who understands the parent’s tools, access and data controls.
  9. Transition or programme manager. A person who tracks knowledge transfer and keeps both sides to the plan.
  10. Early analysts and associates. Hired once managers are in place to train and supervise them.

The exact order will shift with the functions you are moving. The principle holds: leaders and enablers first, then the people who do and teach the work, then volume.

Choosing a hub city

Most GCCs in India are concentrated in a handful of cities, each with a slightly different profile. The right choice depends on the talent you need most.

  • Bengaluru. The deepest ecosystem for technology, data, AI and product talent, with a large community of experienced GCC leaders. Competition for talent is also the most intense.
  • Hyderabad. A major hub for technology, pharma and life sciences, and finance operations, with strong infrastructure for large campuses.
  • Pune. Known for engineering, automotive and manufacturing-linked centres, and a growing finance and analytics base.
  • Chennai. Strong in engineering, banking and financial services operations, and finance and accounting.
  • Delhi NCR (Gurugram and Noida). Large pools of finance, consulting and business services talent, with proximity to corporate head offices.
  • Mumbai. India’s financial capital, with deep banking, financial services, risk and treasury expertise.

Many companies start in one city and later add a second hub for resilience or access to a different skill set.

Working with the global parent: who owns which hiring decision

Many new centres lose time because nobody agreed who decides what. Candidates wait while approvals move between India and head office. Settle ownership before the first search starts. One common split is shown below. Adjust it to your own governance.

Write the split down and share it with everyone on interview panels. Name one person in head office who can approve exceptions quickly.

Common GCC hiring mistakes

  1. Hiring the team before the leader. Without a credible leader, it is harder to attract strong managers, and early hires may not fit the culture you want.
  2. Benchmarking pay against the wrong market. GCC talent compares offers with other GCCs, not with the parent country or with local service firms. Benchmark against the local GCC market for the role and city.
  3. Underestimating notice periods. Notice periods for experienced professionals in India are often long, so plan hiring timelines and start dates accordingly.
  4. Treating diversity as a later phase. Gender balance is far easier to build from the first hires than to correct after the team has grown.
  5. Assessing only technical skill. Success in a GCC depends heavily on communication, stakeholder management across time zones and comfort with ambiguity.

Tip

Ask about notice period and current obligations at the first screening call, not at offer stage. It lets you set realistic start dates and plan the transition around them.

How to choose a GCC hiring partner

Look for a partner with genuine depth in the functions you are hiring for, experience of leadership and confidential searches, and the ability to share market mapping and compensation insight before the shortlist. Ask how they build diverse shortlists and how they will support you beyond the first few hires. Our diversity hiring approach, for example, starts with the first leadership search.

Frequently asked questions

What does GCC stand for?

Global Capability Centre: an offshore unit owned by a global company that delivers functions such as finance, analytics, technology and operations for the parent organisation.

Who should a new GCC hire first?

Usually the site head or centre director and the leaders of the first functions to move. They shape the culture and help attract the managers and specialists who follow.

How is GCC hiring different from IT staffing?

GCC hiring builds permanent teams that belong to the parent company, so cultural fit, leadership and long-term retention matter. IT staffing typically supplies contract resources for projects.

How can a GCC improve gender diversity?

Set goals from the first hire, ask partners for balanced shortlists, use structured interviews, and consider returnship programmes that bring experienced women professionals back into the workforce.

Should the global parent or the India team make hiring decisions?

Usually both, for different decisions. The parent typically owns the headcount plan and the site head appointment. Local leaders own most manager and specialist hires. Agree the split in writing before the first search starts.

Which city is best for a new GCC in India?

It depends on the talent you need most. Bengaluru is strongest for technology, data and AI. Mumbai is strong for banking, risk and treasury. Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai and Delhi NCR each have their own strengths.
